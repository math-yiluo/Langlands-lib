# Connected reductive groups over infinite fields are dominated by a torus

`Langlands.GroupScheme.ReductiveTorusDominationStatement` states that, for
every infinite field `k : Type u` and every actual group scheme `G` over
`Spec k` with `IsConnectedReductive G`, there are an actual group scheme `T`
over `Spec k` with `IsTorus T` and a morphism `f : T.X ⟶ G.X` whose
underlying map of topological spaces has Zariski dense range.

## Conventions

`GroupScheme (Spec (CommRingCat.of k))` is the category of group objects in
`Over (Spec k)`, so `T.X` and `G.X` are schemes over `Spec k`, and `f` is a
morphism in `Over (Spec k)`, that is, a morphism of `k`-schemes. Its
underlying morphism of schemes is `f.left`, and `Dense (Set.range f.left)`
says that `f` is dominant in Milne's sense: its image is dense. The map `f`
need not be a group homomorphism; in the derivation below it is a
multiplication map, which is a homomorphism only when the factors commute.

The hypothesis is the existing `IsConnectedReductive G`: `G.X` is smooth and
affine over `Spec k`, its underlying space is connected, and the base change
of `G` to `AlgebraicClosure k` satisfies `HasTrivialUnipotentRadical`. This is
Milne's notion of a reductive group: "smooth and connected and the unipotent
radical of `G_{k^a}` is trivial" (Section 19, printed p.400, PDF p.416,
recalling 6.46).

- Smooth and affine over a field imply finite type, so `G` is an affine
  algebraic group. A smooth algebraic group is geometrically reduced, which
  is Milne's "group variety" (printed p.12, PDF p.28: "group variety" =
  "geometrically reduced algebraic group").
- Milne 6.46 (printed p.135, PDF p.151) defines the unipotent radical
  `R_u(G)` as the largest connected normal unipotent subgroup variety, and
  calls a connected group variety over `k` reductive if `R_u(G_{k^a})` is
  trivial. `HasTrivialUnipotentRadical` of the base change requires every
  smooth, connected, unipotent, normal closed subgroup of `G_{k^a}` to be
  trivial. That holds exactly when the largest such subgroup is trivial.
- `IsUnipotent` is Milne 6.45 (same page): every nonzero finite-dimensional
  representation has a nonzero fixed vector.

The conclusion uses the existing `IsTorus T`: for some field `K : Type u`
with a `k`-algebra structure, the base change of `T` to `Spec K` is isomorphic
to `splitTorus (Spec K) I` for a finite index type `I`. `splitTorus` is the
finite product of copies of `𝔾_m`. This is Milne's Definition 12.14 (printed
p.236, PDF p.252): a torus is an algebraic group over `k` that becomes
isomorphic to a finite product of copies of `𝔾_m` over some field containing
`k`. Requiring `K : Type u` loses nothing. By Theorem 12.18(d) and Corollary
12.19 (printed pp.237–238, PDF pp.253–254), a torus splits over a separable
closure, and hence over `AlgebraicClosure k : Type u`. `IsTorus` does not
separately require `T` to be of finite type, so the torus asked for is, if
anything, easier to supply.

The statement covers every infinite field, including imperfect ones, every
connected reductive group, split or not, and every universe `u`. It adds no
splitness, perfectness, characteristic, maximal-torus or generation
hypothesis. Rank zero is included: then `G` is trivial, and the
zero-dimensional torus `splitTorus (Spec k) I` with empty `I`, mapped to the
identity point, satisfies the conclusion.

## Provenance

The statement is derived content. Milne does not state it as a numbered
result. It is the structural step in the proof of Corollary 17.92,
specialized to reductive groups as in the proof of Theorem 17.93(b), with the
factors combined into one torus. The sources are J. S. Milne, *Algebraic
Groups* (2017), registered as `milne-2017-algebraic-groups`, and T. A.
Springer, *Linear Algebraic Groups*, second edition (1998), registered as
`springer-1998-linear-algebraic-groups`. Milne's printed page numbers equal
PDF page numbers minus 16, and Springer's equal PDF page numbers minus 14. The
registered errata snapshot `milne-2017-algebraic-groups-errata-2026-11-02`
was read. Its entry for printed p.364 corrects Example 17.42 ("take
I={1,…,n}"), which is not used here. Its entry for printed p.20 corrects the
statement of Proposition 1.45; item 3 explains why the use of Corollary 1.47
is unaffected. It has no entries for printed pp.12, 17, 55–56, 135, 236–238,
379, 381–382 or 400.

1. Theorem 17.91 (printed p.381, PDF p.397): every connected group variety is
   generated by its Cartan subgroups. Milne gives the proof for infinite `k`
   and omits the finite case (Borel–Springer). This is the reason for the
   hypothesis `Infinite k`.
2. 17.43–17.44 (printed p.364, PDF p.380; recalled on printed p.381): the
   Cartan subgroups are the centralizers `C_G(T)` of maximal tori `T`, and
   they are smooth, connected and nilpotent. Corollary 17.84 (printed p.379,
   PDF p.395): in a reductive group, a torus `T` is maximal if and only if
   `C_G(T) = T`. Hence the Cartan subgroups of a reductive group are exactly
   its maximal tori. By Summary 1.36 (printed p.17, PDF p.33), a connected
   algebraic group is geometrically connected, so the base change of a Cartan
   subgroup to `k^a` is a smooth connected closed subgroup of `G_{k^a}`.
3. Generated subgroups. By Proposition 2.51 and the remark after it (printed
   p.56, PDF p.72), a family of maps from geometrically reduced schemes to `G`
   generates a smallest algebraic subgroup, which is smooth, and its formation
   commutes with extension of the base field. By Corollary 1.47 (printed
   p.20, PDF p.36), over a separably closed field `H ↦ H(k)` is a bijection
   from subgroup varieties onto closed subgroups of `G(k)`. The errata's
   corrected 1.45 gives, for a closed subgroup `S` of `G(k)`, a unique
   reduced algebraic subgroup `H` with `|H|` the closure of `S`, `H`
   geometrically reduced and `H(k) = S`. Over an algebraically closed field,
   this is the same bijection. Springer works with linear algebraic groups
   over an algebraically closed field (Chapter 2, printed p.21, PDF p.35).
   His Corollary 2.2.7(i) (printed p.27,
   PDF p.41) says that the subgroup generated by any family `(G_i)_{i ∈ I}` of
   closed connected subgroups is closed and connected and equals a finite
   product `G_{a(1)} ⋯ G_{a(n)}` for some `n ≥ 0` and `a(1), …, a(n) ∈ I`. The
   proof of Corollary 17.92 (printed p.382, PDF p.398) uses this as "a
   dominant morphism `C_1 × ⋯ × C_m → G`" with the `Cᵢ` Cartan subgroups.
4. A finite product of tori is a torus. Each factor splits over a separable
   closure (12.18–12.19), so the product splits there too, since a product of
   split tori is split.

The derivation does not use one sentence in the printed proof of Milne's
Proposition 2.50 (printed p.56): that `φⁿ : Xⁿ → H` is dominant for `n` large.
For a general geometrically reduced `X` with `inv(φ(X)) ⊆ φ(X)` this is
false. Over `ℚ`, the reduced subscheme `X = {1, 2, 1/2}` of `𝔾_m` satisfies
these conditions and generates `𝔾_m`, but each `Im(φⁿ)` is a finite set. The
errata snapshot has no entry for this page. Springer's Proposition 2.2.6
(printed pp.26–27, PDF pp.40–41) has the needed hypotheses: each piece is
irreducible and its image contains `e`. His Exercise 2.2.9(2) (printed p.27)
shows that `e` cannot be omitted. Cartan subgroups are connected subgroups,
so they satisfy both conditions, and Corollary 2.2.7 applies to them.

How the derivation runs. Let `(C_i)_{i ∈ I}` be the Cartan subgroups of `G`.
By 17.91, `G` is the smallest algebraic subgroup containing every `C_i`. By
item 3, `G_{k^a}` is then the smallest algebraic subgroup containing every
`(C_i)_{k^a}`. These are closed connected subgroups (item 2). By Springer
2.2.7(i), the subgroup `H` of `G(k^a)` that they generate is closed, and
`H = C_{a(1)}(k^a) ⋯ C_{a(n)}(k^a)` for some `a(1), …, a(n) ∈ I`. By 1.47,
`H` is the set of points of a subgroup variety `H'` of `G_{k^a}`. Each
`(C_i)_{k^a}` is reduced, and its points, which are dense in it, lie in `H'`.
So `H'` contains every `(C_i)_{k^a}`. Hence `H' = G_{k^a}` and
`H = G(k^a)`. Let `m : C_{a(1)} × ⋯ × C_{a(n)} → G` be the multiplication
morphism `(c_1, …, c_n) ↦ c_1 ⋯ c_n` over `k`. Its base change to `k^a` is
surjective on `k^a`-points. These points are dense in `G_{k^a}`, which is of
finite type over the algebraically closed field `k^a`, so the base change is
dominant. The projection `G_{k^a} → G` is continuous and surjective and maps
the image of the base change into the image of `m`, so `m` is dominant. By
17.84 every `C_{a(j)}` is a torus, so `T := C_{a(1)} × ⋯ × C_{a(n)}` is a
torus by item 4, and `f := m`. If `n = 0`, then `G(k^a)` is trivial, so the
reduced group `G` is trivial, and `T` is the empty product `Spec k`. This is
the rank-zero case above.

This module records the proposition only and supplies no proof. Through
dense-range transport, it is the structural input for
`Langlands.GroupScheme.ReductiveRationalPointDensityStatement` (Milne
17.93(b)), together with the density of rational points of tori (Milne 12.64,
printed p.251).

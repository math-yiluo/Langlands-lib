# Diagonal identification from square-set integrals

This is an internal lemma for the proof of `TraceClassContinuousKernelDiagonalStatement`. It implements the diagonal part of the averaging argument in Brislawn (1991), Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 2.4, using square-set integral identities rather than an almost everywhere expansion of the kernel on the whole product space.

For a finite Borel measure on a second-countable space, suppose `a i` and `b i` are countably indexed square-integrable functions with summable integrated energies `w i = (‖a i‖² + ‖b i‖²) / 2`. The lemma `ae_eq_diagonal_tsum` assumes that the integral of the integrable kernel over every measurable square `A × A` equals the sum of the products of the integrals of `a i` and the conjugate integrals of `b i` over `A`. It proves almost everywhere equality between the supplied diagonal and the diagonal series, assuming continuity at almost every diagonal point.

The finite partition cells eventually lie in every neighborhood of their point. At a point of continuity, this gives convergence of the square-cell averages of the kernel to its supplied diagonal value. Normalizing the square-set integral identity expresses each average as a series of products of cell averages. Jensen's inequality for `‖·‖²` and the arithmetic mean inequality bound each product in norm by the cell average of `w i`. For any finite set `F` of indices, integration commutes with the sum of the energies outside `F`; the average of this tail thus bounds the error of the finite sum on `F`. Lévy upward convergence applies simultaneously to the `a i`, the `b i`, and these countably many integrable energy tails. Passing to the limit in the error bound leaves the pointwise energy tail; this tends to zero as `F` exhausts the indices. No maximal-function estimate is used.

The series helpers use the monotonicity of integrals of nonnegative functions, `lintegral_tsum`, and `enorm_tsum_le_tsum_enorm` to pass from integrability and summable integrals of the norms to integrability of a sum and almost everywhere norm summability.
